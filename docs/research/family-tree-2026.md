# Drawing a family on screen, 2026: what A Story should take

Research note for the founder, October 2026. Quoted phrases are the products' own words from their public pages, help centres and release notes; (reported) marks a fact taken from reviews or forum posts rather than the product's own page.

The founder's verdict on the current tree was "awful awful awful". It is a chart: tinted squares with initials, elbow lines, a zoom toolbar, empty canvas. Their own first drafts lead every screen with a photograph, a terracotta rail with dots, faces with quoted lines. So the question for each product was not "how do they store a pedigree" but: what is the person on screen, how are relationships drawn, how do generations read, what is tappable, how does it behave on a phone, what is the empty state, and does it feel like a family or like software.

## 1. The field

**FamilySearch** (web and the Family Tree app). Five views. Landscape: "You are in the center, descendants are on the left, and ancestors are on the right", a couple box with a "Children" link at the bottom. Portrait: vertical, "Your profile is in the middle of the chart, with your ancestors above you and your descendants below"; the 2025 update added the ability to view siblings by "clicking the arrows on the side of an individual's card", and "Each family line that you open stays on screen until you decide to close it." The "+" on a card offers "Add child" and "Add sibling"; "You can only add a spouse with the + icon if there is no spouse already added." Fan chart: up to seven generations, maternal on the right, paternal on the left, colour-coded by birth country or by how many "sources, stories, and photos" each relative has; it is the view the phone app leads with. Descendancy and First Ancestor (clan genealogies) are web only. Person card: a rounded rectangle, a small round photo or a grey silhouette, the name, "1912–1979", an ID. What works: the fan chart turns the whole ancestry into one shape you can take in at once, and the colour-by-photos view quietly shows where the family is thin. What is clinical: the cards are forms, the lines are wiring, and a tree with no photos is a page of grey silhouettes.

**Ancestry.** Two layouts: horizontal, "shows direct ancestors only", the home person on the left; vertical, "shows everyone in the tree", ancestors above and descendants below. The person is a rounded box with a square photo or silhouette, name and years. Relationships are elbow lines. The famous affordance is the green leaf: hints are "suggestions, not confirmed facts", a new one flagged with a red dot. Selecting a person opens a side panel with Timeline, Family and Hints (reported, 2013) rather than leaving the chart. What works: the leaf makes the tree feel alive, something is always waiting; the side panel keeps you on the drawing. What is clinical: the whole thing is a research desk.

**MyHeritage.** The 2021 redesign of its "most-used family tree view" was "cleaner and more modern": circular photos (reported), sex shown by "a more subtle thin color frame" instead of blue and pink fills (two users found it too faint), a pencil icon to edit, a camera icon "to add a profile photo to anyone without one", a "+" at the bottom of every card opening a "spider menu" for parents, siblings, spouses and children. Empty "Add father" and "Add mother" cards "have been made more narrow to take up less space" and can be hidden. A Generations slider, a "Show cousins of the main person" switch, zoom at bottom right. The side panel shows age, places, "your relationship to the person", photos, and an "Immediate family" list "collapsed by default". A December 2025 webinar adds "a new wizard for adding close relatives". What works: round faces, thin frames, the camera icon that nags only where a face is missing, and the empty parent cards that invite without shouting. What is clinical: the Discoveries, DNA and consistency panels; it is a database with a nicer skin.

**Geni.** Standard view shows "each profile's node, picture (if available) and years of birth and death"; "Flip" nodes reveal places, occupation and an About summary on hover; a five-generation horizontal view and a fan chart; posters of "five generation family trees, including family names, dates, and photos" at 16 by 20 inches (2010). What works: the flip card, one hover for the story behind the face. What is clinical: the "World Family Tree" scale; everyone is a node.

**Family Echo.** The honest minimum. One person to start ("Start your family tree by clicking the pencil icon and entering your name"), then "add parents, children, partners, siblings and more". Everything is a setting: tree Partial, Multiple, Complete or Classic; direction Down, Up, Right or Left; partner "Left or Top"; box colours per sex; line style per relationship (current partners, other partners, non-biological, "friends/pets"). Autosave says "All changes saved." A user with eighty people: it "doesn't look that aesthetic" (reported). What works: the relation words are plain, and friends and pets are allowed in. What is clinical: boxes, every one of them.

**Treemily.** The wall poster as a product: "Family Chart", "Ancestor Treemily", "Descendant Treemily", "Dynasty Treemily", a "Forrest Tool" for several trees on one canvas, "Easy to order prints", turn a tree "into home decor", trees as gifts. The illustrated wall-tree genre they and Etsy sell by the thousand: a drawn trunk and curving branches, round or oval portraits in frames hung on the branches, names in a serif beneath, grandparents at the top or at the roots, the whole thing meant to hang in a hallway. What works: it is the only format in this list that people frame and hang, which means it is the only one that already looks like a family. What is missing: it is static; nothing is tappable and nothing changes when someone joins.

**Gramps Web.** "The free, open-source genealogy system for building your family tree – together", with "two different interactive pedigree views", a Timeline and a Map beside the tree, and mobile support. Desktop Gramps draws "solid lines" for birth relations and "dashed lines" for adoption and step relations. What works: the timeline and the map sit beside the tree as equals. What is clinical: it is a research tool with a web face.

**Kindred.** The iOS app named in the brief could not be pinned down; several apps carry the name. Nearest: "Kindred Moments: Share Stories" (voice memos and videos that become "a living, breathing family archive", "simple, thought-provoking questions") and the UK "Kindred" ("spoken histories, treasured recipes, childhood photos, time-locked messages"). Both show the family as a list of people with photos and recordings, not as a tree.

**Lineage.** Two things carry the name: lineage.tools, a browser builder (free plan "1 family tree, up to 250 people per tree"), and "Lineage: AI Ancestor & DNA", which turns "a single selfie" into an AI-imagined heritage and "let AI imagine the ancestors you never knew". The second is a warning, not a model: nothing synthetic goes near a real family. GensTree, the clan-genealogy app, shipped "updated family tree diagram interface and profile pictures" in January 2026: the whole category is converging on faces.

**Storyworth and Remento** (the direct competitors). Neither draws a tree. Storyworth has "Unlimited storytellers" and "Collaborate on books with family and friends"; relatives "read stories in real time" and comment. Remento: family and friends "add photos, suggest questions, and vote on favorites"; "a secure private archive your whole family can enjoy"; collaborators "follow along and react"; "Each story becomes a chapter in a beautiful hardcover book". The family appears as a row of small round faces on the project and under each story, and as the names on comments. What works: the family is present as voices, not as a diagram; one storyteller is the centre of the page by construction. What is missing: no sense of who is whose; a cousin and a grandmother look the same.

**Apple Photos and Google Photos.** The face is the door. Apple: "You see a face thumbnail for each person or pet your device recognizes"; on the Mac "Faces that you mark as favorites appear in large squares at the top of the window"; naming happens on the tile itself. Google: a "row of faces", "Click a face to find photos of them", and "Only you can find the private face labels you choose". What works: no chart at all, a grid of faces with the important ones larger, one tap to everything about a person. What is missing: relationships; Photos does not know a mother from a neighbour.

**Locket and BeReal.** Locket is "a widget that shows live photos from your best friends, right to your Home Screen", at most twenty friends, "They don't count or track the reactions", praised for "a bit of much-loved skeuomorphic design language" (reported). BeReal: one prompt, a two-minute window, "post late—it still counts", "private by default". What works: faces, few of them, and freshness; you know at a glance who has been around this week. What to leave: the clock.

## 2. The principles for A Story's family

1. **Faces first.** Every product that feels warm leads with the photograph (MyHeritage's circles, Photos' tiles, the wall poster, Remento's row). Every one that feels clinical leads with a box. A person with no photograph should be a cream card with a name, never a tinted square with an initial.
2. **The storyteller at the centre.** Remento and Storyworth get this free because the product has one teller. The tree should too: the storyteller's frame largest, in the middle of her generation, everything else placed relative to her ("Rose's parents", "Rose's children").
3. **Generations readable at a glance.** The wall poster and FamilySearch's portrait view both read top to bottom without a legend. Rows, not a graph; the eye should count four generations before it reads one name.
4. **Warm materials.** The sheet, gold hairlines, white-bordered prints, ink, string. No sex colours (MyHeritage's users could not even see the subtle ones), no zoom toolbar, no empty canvas.
5. **The family's voices present, not just names.** Under a face: "What they remember: 2", and on the person page the family's lines in their own words. This is what no genealogy product has and what the founder's drafts already show.
6. **One tap to a person.** The face is the link (Photos). Hover may show the caption; a tap opens the page.
7. **Phone first.** FamilySearch leads its app with the fan chart because a pedigree does not fit a phone; Remento and Photos are phone-native because they are lists and grids. A Story's family must read on a 375px screen without pinching.

## 3. What to steal, product by product

- **MyHeritage:** the round portrait with a thin frame, the camera icon only where a face is missing, the narrow "Add father / Add mother" cards that can be hidden, the "+" that opens four relations in one menu.
- **FamilySearch:** the "+" that offers only the relations that make sense ("Add child", "Add sibling", a spouse only if there is none), opened lines that stay open, siblings behind an arrow rather than on screen.
- **Ancestry:** the side panel that keeps you on the drawing; the leaf, translated as a quiet mark on someone with a question waiting for them.
- **Geni:** the flip card, as the caption under the pointer.
- **Family Echo:** the plain relation words, friends allowed in.
- **Treemily and the wall posters:** the composition itself, frames on branches, grandparents at the top, the whole thing worth hanging.
- **Gramps Web:** the timeline and the map as equals to the tree.
- **Storyworth and Remento:** the family as voices; the storyteller as the page.
- **Apple and Google Photos:** the face as the door; the important faces larger.
- **Locket:** few faces, large, fresh; a sense of who has been here this week without a count.

Do not copy: sex colours, hint badges with numbers, DNA and record panels, AI-imagined relatives, zoom toolbars as the primary control, a canvas that opens mostly empty.
